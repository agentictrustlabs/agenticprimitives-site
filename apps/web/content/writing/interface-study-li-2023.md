## The study and why we depend on it

**S. Li, J. Luo and T. Ye, “Investigation of Reducing Interface State Density in 4H-SiC by Increasing Oxidation Rate”, Nanomaterials 13, 1568 (2023), doi:10.3390/nano13091568.**

The one source in our library that measures composition across the SiC/oxide interface (challenge system 3, target composition profile). Figure 8 gives the carbon and oxygen atomic fractions from a STEM-EDS line scan across the interface for a sample oxidized dry at 1400 °C for 13 min and annealed in NO, with and without a phosphorus implant before oxidation. The paper also gives the oxide thickness of three samples (Figure 3) and interface state densities (Figure 6). The predictor has no program for a composition profile; this page records the profile, runs the thickness and interface-state programs on the stated history, and says what a carbon program would have to contain. A candidate carbon program, written in the language but not added to the predictor, is described in the alignment section.

## How well can this study be reproduced?

**Moderate for thickness, Low for the carbon profile.** The oxidation itself is stated (dry O2, 1400 °C, 13 min) and so is the NO anneal (1200 °C, 70 min). Everything around them is not: the sacrificial oxidation, the field oxide, how a wafer entered and left a 1400 °C tube, the transfer to the NO anneal, the polysilicon deposition. The carbon profile (Figure 8) is an EDS line scan of a FIB lamella in arbitrary position units, with C at 0.36 in SiC where it should be 0.50; which samples it shows is inferred from the text. No program we hold predicts a composition profile, and the language cannot write the NO anneal or the implant.

### What the second reading changed

- There is no stored recipe or dataset for this paper (ontology/library.json: status skimmed, used by nothing). Everything here is from the second reading; nothing contradicts an earlier encoding because there is none.
- SOURCE.md says “carbon accumulation at the interface is reduced by the implantation” from the abstract. Figure 8 shows no carbon excess above the SiC level on either sample: the “accumulation” is a wider transition (3.6 against 2.7 nm, digitised), not a peak.
- SOURCE.md and the search notes give overlaps of about 6 and 3 nm; those are the paper's words. The digitised 90 to 10% carbon falls are 3.6 and 2.7 nm and the oxygen rises 3.5 and 2.8 nm, so the ratio is 1.3, not 2.
- The EDS fractions are not stoichiometric: carbon in SiC reads 0.36 (0.50 expected), oxygen in the oxide 0.61 (0.67), carbon in the oxide 0.03 (background). Only shapes and widths can be compared, not levels. Silicon is not plotted.
- The predictor's consumed-substrate ratio (0.46, STATUS hypothesis, “no source in our library states it”) is stated by this paper: 1:2.17, i.e. 0.461.

```isl
# ==============================================================================
# ISL MODEL: DRY THERMAL OXIDATION OF 4H-SiC (growth sector on a second material)
#
# The OPERATOR and OBSERVATION_MODEL blocks below are copied character for character
# from experiments/vitkavage_dit/oxide_growth.isl.template, the silicon program; a test
# checks that they are identical. Only the declared parameters differ.
#
# Song et al. (2004) fit their SiC data with the same linear-parabolic law, reading its
# two constants as including out-diffusion of the carbon product. The constants here are
# Arrhenius fits to that paper's Tables I and II, per crystal face
# (scripts/build_song_dataset.py). Nothing is fitted by us.
# Fields in {{braces}} are filled by scripts/run_song_sic.py.
# ==============================================================================
SAMPLE {sample_id} {{
    FAMILY: SiC_SiO2
    ORIENTATION: 4H_SiC_{face}_face
}}
STATE x_ox {{
    UNIT: cm
    INITIAL: x_i
    MIN: 0
    MAX: 1.0e-3
}}
CONTROL T {{ UNIT: K }}
CONTROL p_O2 {{ UNIT: atm }}
PARAMETER B0 {{ VALUE: {B0}; UNIT: cm^2 s^-1; STATUS: sourced; SOURCE: {source} }}
PARAMETER E_B {{ VALUE: {E_B}; UNIT: eV; STATUS: sourced; SOURCE: {source} }}
PARAMETER BA0 {{ VALUE: {BA0}; UNIT: cm s^-1; STATUS: sourced; SOURCE: {source} }}
PARAMETER E_BA {{ VALUE: {E_BA}; UNIT: eV; STATUS: sourced; SOURCE: {source} }}
# The constants above are already specific to the crystal face, so no further factor is applied.
PARAMETER r_or {{ VALUE: 1.0; UNIT: 1; STATUS: constant }}
# The paper sets the time offset to zero and reports a native oxide of about 2 angstrom.
PARAMETER x_i {{ VALUE: 2.0e-8; UNIT: cm; STATUS: sourced; SOURCE: Song et al., J. Appl. Phys. 95, 4953 (2004), native oxide about 2 angstrom, agent-read }}
PARAMETER p_ref {{ VALUE: 1.0; UNIT: atm; STATUS: constant }}
PARAMETER k_B {{ VALUE: 8.617333262e-5; UNIT: eV K^-1; STATUS: constant }}
CONTROL_TRAJECTORY {trajectory_id} {{
    RUN Oxidation DURATION {duration_s} TEMP {T_C}->{T_C} AMBIENT dry_O2
}}
# dx/dt = B / (2x + A), with A = B / (B/A).
OPERATOR F_LinearParabolicGrowth : OxideGrowth {{
    EQUATION: d(x_ox)/dt = (p_O2 / p_ref) * B0 * exp(-E_B / (k_B * T)) / (2 * x_ox + B0 * exp(-E_B / (k_B * T)) / (r_or * BA0 * exp(-E_BA / (k_B * T))))
}}
OBSERVATION_MODEL Obs_Oxide_Thickness {{
    TARGET: Thickness
    UNIT: cm
    EQUATION: Thickness = x_ox
}}
EVALUATION_CONTRACT {contract_id} {{
    FROZEN: true
    DATASET: data/derived/song_2004_sic_thickness.csv
    DATASET_DIGEST: sha256:{dataset_sha256}
    SPLIT: {split}
{targets}
}}
```

## Aligning the simulation: what the unstated things would have to be

**The carbon profile has no program, and a candidate shows why carbon in the oxide is not what Figure 8 measures.** A program written in the language (`sic_carbon_profile.isl`, in the session's drafts, not added to the predictor) grows the oxide by the SiC growth law, emits a fraction ν_C of the carbon in the SiC consumed into the oxide at the interface (Hijikata's Si and C emission model, Eq. 10), lets it diffuse with Hijikata's D_C (Table 1: 3.8 × 10⁻¹¹ cm² s⁻¹ at 1400 °C), be oxidized inside the oxide at Hijikata's κ′ rates, and leave at the surface. The grid travels with the interface (FIELD, FLUX with `upwind` for the oxide carried away, BOUNDARY flux at the interface and the surface). With ν_C = 0.1 (Hijikata's Figure 6, Si face near 1100 °C; not measured at 1400 °C) it gives, at the end of the 13 min: 7.6 × 10¹⁵ C cm⁻² emitted, 99.9% of it out through the surface, 6.7 × 10¹² oxidized inside the oxide, 3.5 × 10¹² held; a carbon fraction of 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ on the oxide side of the interface (an upper bound: the grid's far end, 60 nm, stands in for a surface that is nearer while the oxide grows). After the 70 min at 1200 °C nothing is left (10⁻⁴⁴). The carbon crosses 52 nm of oxide in about a second. The result is linear in ν_C: even ν_C = 0.5 gives 9 × 10⁻⁵, 300 times below the EDS floor of 0.03. So the program predicts a step in carbon at the interface, the same for both samples, and the measured 3.6 and 2.7 nm widths must come from the SiC side of the interface, from roughness on the 4° off-cut, or from the probe and the lamella.

**What a program must contain to predict Figure 8:** (1) the moving SiC/SiO2 front, with a growth law that holds at 1400 °C; (2) carbon on the SiC side: emission into the SiC (Hijikata's Figure 2), the diffusivity of carbon interstitials in SiC and a term that holds carbon near the front (clusters, a transition layer; Hijikata's 1.5 nm interface layer). No source in our library gives these constants; (3) a term by which implant damage changes the front (an amorphized layer that oxidizes faster); (4) the NO anneal: nitrogen incorporation and carbon removal; (5) an observation that turns the fields into an EDS atomic fraction across a lamella: the profile convolved with the probe and projection width, plus the off-cut roughness; (6) a grid whose length follows the oxide thickness. Items 1, 2 and 4 are FIELD, FLUX and BOUNDARY statements the language has; 3, 5 and 6 are not writable today.

**The thickness at 1400 °C** needs constants that hold there; the alignment's 25 extra minutes in oxygen are a symptom, not a furnace cycle.

### What our language and ontology cannot yet say

- **Nitric oxide (and N2O, NH3) as gases.** The NO anneal at 1200 °C for 70 min is a stated step the language cannot write; it is an EVENT here. `mix:NO=1.0` would let it be simulated by a program that reads `p_NO` (nitrogen at the interface, Figure 9; the interface-state reduction, Figure 6).
- **An implant statement.** `IMPLANT P ENERGY 30 keV DOSE 1e13 [THROUGH FieldOxide]` as a history step, carrying the species, the projected range and the damage a program can read. The paper's central variable is a sample field no program reads.
- **A profile observable with the instrument in it.** The composition target has no observation form: an `OBSERVATION_MODEL` returns one number. A profile result (value against position from the interface) and a reduction such as `convolve(F, w)` with a stated probe width, sampled at positions, would let Figure 8 be scored and would separate the instrument from the interface.
- **A grid whose length follows a state** (the oxide thickness), so a surface sink sits at the surface while the oxide grows; today the candidate program's sink is at a fixed 60 nm and its carbon is an upper bound.
- **OFFCUT read by programs.** The 4° off-cut is recorded and ignored; it sets the step density that roughens the interface the EDS line crosses, and Song's constants are on axis.
