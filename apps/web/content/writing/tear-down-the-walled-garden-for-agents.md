# Your personal agent is the new walled garden

> **The answer is not a better platform. It is an agent that holds none of your keys.** It acts as your delegate, under permissions you sign and can revoke one at a time. Your credentials prove facts without ever being shown. Every receipt is yours to carry away. We built this. Today an organization's own agent issues the credential, and a member proves it at a live door and pays her dues without the organization learning which member she is.

In September 2025 Coin Center published *Tear Down this Walled Garden: American Values and Digital Identity*. Its argument is simple and, I think, right: identity infrastructure that one intermediary controls — a platform, a bank consortium, a government agency — centralizes surveillance, limits what anyone else can build on it, and lets the gatekeeper privilege its own services. The alternative the report calls an *Open Commons*: credentials a person holds and carries, facts proved rather than documents shown, open standards that no one captures. The report ends with a line worth keeping: *a state-run walled garden is no better than a private park.*

A year later the walled gardens are being built, and they are not identity wallets. They are personal agents.

In the space of seven weeks this autumn, three of the largest companies on earth shipped a program that acts for you. Meta's **Muse** (September 8) books travel, fills forms and checks out purchases; it runs on what Meta calls a *Muse Secure VM*, a private machine that holds both the agent and your personal data. OpenAI's **dots** (DevDay, September 29) are always-on agents, each on its own cloud computer with its own browser, connected to four thousand apps, working after you log off. xAI and Cursor's **Grok Bot**, in beta since August, is reported to sign in to your mail and your CRM from a dedicated computer of its own and keep going while you are away. These are remarkable products. They are also, every one of them, a private park: the agent *and* your data *and* the record of what it did live on the platform's machine, under the platform's account, governed by the platform's trust in you.

This essay is about what identity, authority and privacy have to look like when the holder of a credential is a program rather than a person. That is not a premise about the future. Programs hold credentials today, everywhere: every API key, service-account file, bot token and long-lived session cookie is a credential held by software with nobody at the keyboard. What they hold is a *secret*, and whoever has the secret *is* the identity — it cannot say who authorized the act, cannot be narrowed to one task, cannot be revoked per call, and leaks as easily as it is copied. The personal-agent platforms do not introduce the program as holder; they put your whole life behind that kind of holder. So the question is not whether programs should hold credentials, but what a credential has to be when they do — and what we have built, in the open, so that a person's agent can act for her without becoming a new gatekeeper's property. I will respond to Coin Center's principles directly, because they are the best checklist anyone has written, and I will be honest about where we fail them today.

## The holder is no longer a human with a phone

Everything in the digital-identity literature, Coin Center's report included, carries one quiet assumption: the holder of a credential is a person, holding a device, present at the moment of use. Prove you are over 21 — tap the phone. Prove you are a U.S. person — tap the phone. The zero-knowledge proof is generated by a key the person holds, and the privacy guarantees follow from that.

The personal-agent platforms break the assumption on day one. Muse pays for things while you are in a meeting. A dot files your expense report at 3 a.m. For an agent to present a credential on your behalf, something has to hold the key the credential is bound to, and there are only two obvious answers.

**Give the agent the key.** Now the agent is the holder, cryptographically. This is how every program holds a credential today — the API key in the environment, the service account's JSON file, the bot's token — and the personal agent inherits the pattern whole. Every principle in Coin Center's list is defeated by construction: whoever controls the agent's machine controls every credential it holds, can present any of them to anyone, forever, and the person can only revoke by asking the platform nicely. This is the walled-garden answer, and it is the one the platforms will take by default, because it is the one that works tonight.

**Keep the key on the person's phone.** Now the agent cannot act while she sleeps, which is the entire point of an agent. This is the answer the identity literature implicitly gives, and it is why that literature has nothing to say about agents.

There is a third answer, and it is the one piece of this design that no current standard has written down. We call it **delegated presentation**.

The credential is bound at issuance to a *device key* that is custodied **for** the person — a P-256 key held by the estate's key service inside a confidential machine, under her custody root. It is never the agent's key, never a secret the agent's runtime holds, and never a key on her account: it cannot sign a transaction or an operation for her, only a presentation. The person then signs one **standing wire**: a scoped, revocable delegation from her agent to her runtime that says, in effect, *you may cause my device key to sign presentations — but only to these verifiers, only for these predicate programs, only under these issuer keys, and only for this long*. When a verifier challenges her agent, the agent runs the proof and, inside the one callback where a signature is needed, a **presentation release** judges the wire against the exact statement being proved — audience, predicate program, issuer facet — and signs only if it holds. A wire for another venue signs nothing. The key service checks the same thing a second time: it signs only when the request carries evidence naming the digest, the venue and the predicate the release judged, so a runtime holding the service's credential cannot make a signature no wire covers. The proving system never sees the key; it sees a callback that may refuse.

What this buys is best stated as the differences:

| | holder is a human with a phone | holder is the agent | delegated presentation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| who can produce a proof | only the person, synchronously | the agent, for anything, forever | the agent, for the predicates and audiences the wire names, until revoked |
| compromise of the runtime | nothing | every credential the agent holds is spendable | signatures only under a live wire; the person revokes one wire |
| what the verifier sees | a proof | a proof | a proof — and nothing more; the wire never leaves her estate |
| what the person can prove later | "I presented" | nothing | a receipt: *this* presentation was made under *this* mandate for *this* challenge |
| revocation | issuer | issuer | issuer (the credential) and person (the wire), independently |

It is the rule the whole substrate is built on — *the key is a delegate, never the identity* — applied to a credential's device key. Programs stay credential holders; what changes is that the program holds a delegation it can only exercise, never a secret it owns. And it is what we would contribute to Coin Center's proposed standards body, the John Hancock Project, if it existed tomorrow.

## What we built, and what it looks like at a door

This is not a position paper. It is implemented, end to end, in the published packages and the product repositories that run on them, and a live gate holds it against a real door: a nameless visitor proves "over 18, and I am a member of your organization" at another estate's boundary, in zero knowledge, and is admitted. The membership is a claim in a credential the organization's own agent issued — an act of the organization, under a mandate its steward signed — proven the same way as the age, naming nobody: the door learns that the organization issued her a membership, and not which member. On that admission she pays her dues, from her own treasury, and the organization learns that a member paid.

The pieces, in the order a visit uses them:

- **The issuer publishes a facet, not a trust relationship.** An issuing agent — an organization or a service, never a person — signs a key facet and anchors it as an attestation on its own chain. A verifier anywhere resolves the facet by key id and re-verifies it *through the chain*: the signature answers under the issuer's account, the attestation is live. The issuer is never asked. That is Coin Center's "no phone home," made structural.
- **The credential is proved about, not shown.** We use OpenAC, the Ethereum Foundation's zkID work: predicates over a selectively disclosable credential, proven in zero knowledge, with the issuer's key and the holder's device key hidden inside the proof. A verifier learns that a predicate held. It learns no claim value, no credential, and no key.
- **The verifier asks its own question.** A door builds the proof request from *its* policy and *its* single-use challenge, never from anything the presenter sent. The predicate program is compiled to a canonical form and digested; that digest is what the person's wire names and what the receipt cites.
- **The visitor is nameless, but not actor-less.** The agent knocks as a *pairwise account* — one derived from the person's secret and the door's origin, different at every door, underivable without the secret. The door's admission rules still see a signed actor, which is what an audit needs; they never see the person.
- **One visit per audience, by a window.** Each presentation carries a nullifier, deterministic per device key and audience, which the door's own store consumes atomically. The same person cannot be admitted twice in the window; two doors cannot link her. The door's policy says how long the window is.
- **Revocation lives on neutral ground.** The issuer anchors the root of its *live* credential set on a registry no estate controls — the same registry our estates use for their authority projections. A holder presents a membership proof against the root the verifier *reads from the chain*; a credential the issuer has retired is simply absent from the next root, and every old status proof is stale. Nobody phones a status list.
- **The receipt names the evidence and nothing else.** The door's record of a predicate admission carries the facet, the predicate digest, the nullifier's grade, the status sequence it read, where the proof was verified, and the pairwise account. Never a claim. Never the person's agent. The holder's own receipt, in her vault, adds the one thing the door cannot know: the mandate the presentation was made under.

## Two kinds of anonymous credential, and a message between two agents

The packages carry two presentation suites over the same credential. Both keep the person's account off the wire; they differ in what the verifier learns and in whether two verifiers can compare notes.

**Selective disclosure** (`sd-jwt`). The holder reveals the claims the verifier asked for — the claim *values* — and withholds the rest as digests. She presents under a pairwise pseudonym, so the same verifier recognizes her next time and a different verifier gets a different name. But the issuer's signature travels with every presentation, and it is the same bytes each time, so two verifiers who pool their logs can tell they saw one credential. This is the suite for a verifier that is *entitled* to the values: the organization's own agent, which needs the membership number to post dues against the right ledger.

**Zero-knowledge predicates** (`openac-sd-jwt-p256`). The holder proves that a statement over the claims holds — *over 18 and a member* — and reveals no value, no credential, no issuer signature and no device key. The verifier learns that the statement is true and which issuer stands behind it. Nothing in the proof links two presentations. This is the suite for a verifier that is entitled to a *fact*, which is most verifiers.

A request names one suite and gets that suite or a refusal. There is no ladder from the second down to the first; a verifier that asked for predicates never finds itself holding a birth date.

## What is live today: the organization issues, and a member pays without saying which

This is the run as it happened in October 2026, on the second town's chain. Three parties: a member, her organization's steward, and the organization's own agent. Nobody runs a script with a key in it; every act is an agent's, under a signature a person gave, and every record lands in the vault of whoever it belongs to.

1. **The member asks for her credential through her own agent.** She tells her agent to ask the organization for a membership credential. Her agent checks with the organization's own agent that she is on its roster, reads the *public* half of her device key from her vault, and leaves a request in the organization's vault. The request carries that public key and nothing else; the private half is never read for this.
2. **The organization issues, as itself.** The steward asks the organization's agent to issue, vouching for one extra claim — the member's age. The act is the organization's: it runs under a mandate the steward signs as the organization, with a second approval parked in between. The agent reads the roster (someone the roster does not name is refused in words, and is never admitted by this act), reads the issuer facet the organization published, builds the credential in the facet's claim layout bound to the key her request named, and asks the key service to sign. The issuing key is a P-256 key in the organization's estate with one role: it signs only under evidence naming the credential's digest, the facet and the type, and it cannot present. A device key, for its part, cannot issue. The organization records what it issued — digest, facet, type, member, the key's thumbprint — and never the claim values. The steward's transcript holds the digest, not her claims.
3. **The organization vouches on neutral ground, by itself.** The act writes the credential's digest, blinded, into the organization's own vault as a submission to its live-credential row. A scheduled projector reads that vault, rebuilds the root and anchors it on the federation registry — with a key held per issuer, so an issuer's key can never re-anchor an estate's roots. No operator runs a command; the row moved to its next sequence within the minute.
4. **The credential lands in her vault, by her own grant.** The organization holds no authority over her vault and needs none: the credential is written into her own object by her own grant, the way a payee's receipt is. Her agent can present it; the organization can revoke it; neither can read the other's copy.
5. **She proves at the door, and the door sells her a ticket.** Her agent knocks at the estate's door as a pairwise account and proves `age ≥ 18 ∧ member ≥ 1` in zero knowledge — no value, no credential, no issuer signature, no device key. The door verifies in the container beside it, resolves the facet through the chain, checks the status proof against the root it reads from the registry, consumes the nullifier, and admits. On that admission, and only on one in which the predicates held, it issues a payment ticket: a reference bound to the admission receipt, the organization's treasury, the asset, the amount and the chain. Which organization's terms to ticket the door reads from its *own* receipt — the issuer whose facet the proof was made under — never from the client.
6. **She pays from her own treasury, under a wire she signed once.** Her agent pays the ticket from her treasury to the organization's treasury on the organization's own chain. Her treasury is linked to her in her own private records; it has no public name and no edge on any chain, and her agent finds it anyway, because what is hers is in her tier. The authority is a standing dues wire she signed at her Home: this payee, this much per payment, this much over thirty days. Each payment is a child mandate derived from that wire and signed by her agent, and her own harness verifies every link before value moves. The same intent repeated moves nothing.
7. **The organization accepts, and learns only that a member paid.** The door reads the transfer on the chain, settles the ticket once, and records the acceptance beside the predicate admission. A forged ticket is refused; a settled one cannot settle again. The organization's ledger says a member over eighteen paid dues. Which member is in her receipt, in her vault, with the mandate it was made under.

Two live gates hold this against the running federation and are re-run on a schedule: the predicate visit (twenty-five checks, five of them refusals: a reused nullifier, a foreign venue, a revoked credential, a replayed challenge, a facet outside the door's trust set) and the member payment (fifteen checks, four refusals: no ticket without an admission, one settlement per ticket, a forged reference, a repeated intent). The issuing act has its own refusals: a non-member, an organization with no facet, a vault that will not take the delivery — each said in words, none silently worked around.

The same admission path carries a *message* rather than a knock: an organization's agent that reads only from adult members, without learning which member wrote, asks the same predicate question, holds the presentation under a reference, verifies before it reads, and hands the steward a message from a pairwise account. That variant uses nothing the door does not, and no gate exercises it yet; the predicate half of it is what the door proves every night.

## The seven "nos," scored honestly

Coin Center's principles are a checklist we can be graded against. Here is the grade today, failures included.

| # | principle | where we stand |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **No back door** — user control by cryptographic design | Custody is the person's: passkeys, a signing wallet, or trustees with a quorum. Every disclosure is a grant her agent set. Her credential's device key is held by the key service and signs only under evidence of what her wire covered; the organization's issuing key signs only under evidence of what it is issuing, and neither key can do the other's job. |
| 2 | **No phone home** — nobody reconstructs the usage graph | Pairwise accounts per verifier, zero-knowledge predicates, issuer keys checked against the chain rather than the issuer, receipts that are commitments only, and a public knowledge base that may hold *only* what the chain itself would reveal. The key service's own audit row names the venue and the predicate digest beside the key id; it names no claim and no person. |
| 3 | **No chokepoint** — present at will | The presentation runs in the person's own runtime; no platform mediates it. She asks for the credential through her own agent, and the organization's agent issues it as the organization — no issuing service in between. A person can run her own Home: it is decentralized and under her control, and a shared Home is a convenience, never a mandate. Portability of the vault and recovery of the account are what let her move between them. |
| 4 | **No honeypot** — no mass-theft target | One vault per person with per-record scope, no raw key at rest, one tenant per estate. A door's receipts name a pairwise account and nothing else. |
| 5 | **No leaks** — network-level privacy | Open. Edge workers see addresses and timing. We say so rather than claim otherwise. |
| 6 | **No dead zones** — offline capability | The proof's expensive half is prepared offline by construction; the agent itself needs its runtime. Named, not solved. |
| 7 | **No lockout** — resilient recovery | This is where we are furthest ahead: trustees, guardian quorums, a 48-hour window the owner can cancel, and an agent address that never changes through any of it. |

The policy half of the report — standards, certification of issuers, safe harbors for reliance, developer protection, credential minimization — maps onto things we already ship as *evidence*: issuer certification is an anchored facet; safe-harbor reliance is a verification receipt the institution can show; minimization is a per-grant setting on the person's agent, a ladder from plaintext down to *predicate only* that she chooses.

## Identity for agents: an address, not a name, and never a score

Coin Center writes about the identity of people. The agentic web needs an answer for the identity of agents, and here the field is converging on exactly the mistake the report warns against: a registry that is also the authority, a name that is also the identity, and a *reputation score* that is also a permission.

Our answer has three parts.

**Every agent is a smart account.** A person's agent, an organization's, a service's — each *is* an on-chain account address, and that address is the canonical identifier. Names, agent cards, registry entries, DNS records and discovery listings are *projections* of it: delete a listing and you have lost a listing, not an identity. Credentials rotate; the address does not. When a credential is lost, the person replaces the key and keeps the address, through a recovery her trustees can see and she can cancel.

**Resolution is not authority.** Finding an agent — its endpoint, its card, its current keys — answers only "may this party discover how to reach it," never "may this party use it." A nameless agent is not an unresolvable one; a person's agent can be reachable by exactly the parties she granted, under grants bound to the recipient, and invisible to everyone else. The public graph that any town publishes holds nothing a stranger could not reproduce from the chain.

**Reputation is six signals, never one number.** When one agent resolves another in a context, the resolver returns the subject, the context, the relationship, the bindings, the authority and the evidence as *separate* signals. No gate anywhere reads a score. Attestations — endorsements, standing in a town, a steward's charter — are opt-in, signed by both parties, revocable by either, and read as evidence. Where the field's new registries publish reputation feedback, we treat the registry entry as one more facet of the address and the feedback as a discovery signal, never a permission. Coin Center's own example of a *reputation threshold proof* fits this exactly: the subject proves, in zero knowledge, that attestations she chooses to cite cross a line — the verifier learns that they do, and nothing about which.

## Three scales, one rule

The products that run on these packages are organized by scale, because the privacy argument changes at each one.

**Home** (`ap-home`) is where a person, an organization or a service governs its own agent: the runtime that turns an ask into an intent, a mandate, a plan, per-step verification and a receipt; the vault that is the record; the admission edge in front of them; and a Home MCP that lets Claude.ai or any other client reach the person's agent *as the person*, under a delegation she can revoke, never under a bearer token. The rule on every screen: being a participant does not authorize an action; the requested effect must be within the acting principal's current, scoped delegation.

**Town** (`ap-town`) is one chain, every estate on it, and the services they share — a registry, a public graph, naming, chain access, a portal. Nothing in a town grants anything. A name resolves, a registry lists, the graph holds what the chain can prove, and an estate's authority stays in the estate.

**Federation** (`ap-federation`) is where two towns interoperate over proof rather than trust: a registry on neutral ground that no estate controls, where estates anchor the roots of what they vouch for and issuers anchor the roots of what they still stand behind; doors that admit a visitor on a leaf proved against a root *read from that registry*; and now the predicate grade, where the visitor proves a fact about a credential and the door learns the fact, not the visitor.

This is the Open Commons the report asks for, built from the agent's side rather than the wallet's. Muse, dots and Grok Bot could stand at any of these doors tomorrow. The question is whether they would present as the person's *delegate*, under a wire she holds, or as the person — holding her keys, speaking in her name, keeping her receipts on a machine she cannot carry away.

## What a personal-agent platform should do

If I could put five sentences in front of the teams shipping Muse, dots and Grok Bot, they would be these.

1. **Hold the agent as a delegate, never as the identity.** The person's agent is an account she controls; your service holds a revocable delegation to act through it. When she leaves, she takes the account, the receipts and the credentials with her.
2. **Approval is a signature, never an assertion.** The emerging Personal Agent Protocol lets a company be told `approved_by: user`, and says itself that this works on trust. Let the confirmation *be* the signature over the specific act, bound to its intent, so the company holds proof rather than a claim.
3. **The person is never the `client_id`.** Your service is the client at the company's door; the person is a pairwise subject there, and nameless by default. She should be able to prove she is over 21, or a resident, or in good standing, without the door learning which person's agent asked.
4. **Publish facets, accept predicates.** If you issue anything — a membership, a verified-account status — anchor the issuing key where a stranger can check it, and accept a zero-knowledge predicate at your own doors so that the next platform's users can prove things to yours.
5. **Let her revoke one wire.** Not "sign out everywhere." One permission, for one venue and one kind of statement, gone in one transaction, with the agent otherwise unchanged.

None of this requires a chain the public cannot read, a registry one company runs, or a wallet one company ships. It requires the keys to be delegates, the proofs to be evidence, and the receipts to belong to the person. The walled garden is not the agent. It is the decision to make the agent the holder.

## Where this stands, and what is still owed

The suite, the delegated presentation, the issuer row, the nullifier and the door's predicate grade are shipped in the published packages and live in the federation; the gate that proves them runs nightly. When this essay was first drafted, three things were not done. Two have since closed. The person's device key was a local signer standing in for a managed one, because the key service we run spoke only the Ethereum curve; it now holds a P-256 key kind, signs presentations only under evidence naming the digest, the venue and the predicate, and refuses to sign anything for her account. And the issuer was the estate's registry agent standing in for the organization; now the organization's own agent issues, as an act under its steward's mandate, vouches on its own anchored row, and delivers into the member's vault. The list below sorts what remains by what it is: a gap, a deployment, or a property we carry another way.

The design, the specifications and the ontology that binds every one of these records to a declared class are public. If you are building a personal agent, build it so that the person could leave. If you are writing the standard, write the holder as a program acting under a mandate. And if you are Coin Center: thank you for the checklist. Here is the grade; the red cells are where we are working.

## The open items, sorted by what they are

Three kinds of item, and the kind is said on each. A **gap** is something the design or the protocol does not yet do. **Infrastructure** means the design is done and what remains is to deploy or configure more of it. **Carried otherwise** means we do not close the gap as stated, because another mechanism gives the property and we say which. Each item also says whether the limit is OpenAC's or ours, and what it would add to the bill. The pilot today runs on one confidential VM (about $280–330 a month), Key Vault Premium (about $40–60), a burstable database, and Cloudflare Workers whose cost at this volume rounds to nothing.

**Nullifier.** *Kind: a gap in OpenAC, carried otherwise.* The value a door consumes is asserted by the holder — a signature over a fixed string with the device key, hashed. An honest holder produces the same value every time; a dishonest one running a different stack is not bound to it. The core specification lists nullifiers among the things it does not define, and the `Show` circuit emits no value derived from the device key; the sibling proof-of-personhood specification does it in-circuit, which is the shape OpenAC could adopt. *How we carry it:* the receipt records the grade as *asserted* so no reader mistakes it; in our own estates the device key sits in the key service and signs only under bound evidence, which narrows the dishonest-holder case to someone outside our stack; and the derivation uses a signature rather than the private scalar, so the in-circuit form can arrive without the key ever leaving custody. When the circuit ships, the switch is a policy flip. *Cost to close:* none to us; new verifying keys upstream.

**Status proof.** *Kind: a gap in OpenAC, carried otherwise.* The membership proof against the issuer's live-credential root reveals which leaf, so it links to the credential; a door asks for it only where the alternative is phoning a status list. Revocation is not in the profile and the circuit takes no status input. *How we carry it:* the root is anchored on neutral ground and read from the chain, so the issuer still learns nothing, and the row is shaped for the zero-knowledge form. *Cost to close:* none to us; upstream circuit work.

**Key custody.** *Kind: infrastructure.* The design is done and enforced: the key service's platform root is released only through attestation — Key Vault hands the key that seals it only to a genuine, Azure-compliant, non-debuggable AMD SEV-SNP confidential VM with our VM's id, wrapped to a fresh key inside that machine's TPM. A copy of the sealed root anywhere else releases nothing. The issuing key and the device key are the service's, each with one role, each signing only under evidence of what was judged. *What remains to deploy:* the release binds the machine, not the software on its disk, so proving which binary runs needs a measured, immutable OS image whose boot measurements enter the policy; then a second node in a second zone; then a quorum of two operators for the commands that change who may ask. Every restart today is a short boot ceremony that opens egress to the attestation service for a minute. *Cost so far:* one HSM key, about a dollar a month, and cents of network per boot.

**The second estate's device keys.** *Kind: infrastructure.* The first estate's member presents with a device key the key service holds. The second estate's member — the one in the run above — presents with a local signer standing in, because her estate's tenant has no P-256 device key yet. Creating one is a key-service ceremony and a re-issue; there is nothing to design. *Cost to close:* an afternoon.

**Membership across estates.** *Kind: a gap, ours.* An organization issues to its own members, and the member in the run above lives in the organization's estate. A visitor from *another* estate who is a member here cannot yet be invited and recorded as one: an invitation to another deployment no longer carries anyone's session, and joining writes the membership at the joiner's own deployment. Until that lands — an invitation over the agent protocol under an app delegation, and an organization recording a member from another chain — the estate's registry agent stands in as issuer for exactly that visitor, and the register says so in those words. *Cost to close:* design work in the runtime; no infrastructure.

**A person's agent at another service.** *Kind: half closed, half infrastructure.* When a person's agent asked a service hosted elsewhere, the routed ask carried her Home session token to that host. It no longer does: over the wire an ask carries the app delegation it was admitted under, or nothing, and a service that wants a stable pseudonym declares a pairwise asker it can admit without any credential at all. *What remains to deploy:* the pairwise asker is written and tested and waits on a package release and the Home wiring that derives the pseudonym from her vault. *Cost to close:* a release.

**Network privacy.** *Kind: a gap, ours and everyone's.* Edge workers see the sender's address and timing; it is outside what any credential protocol can fix. We say so. The design candidates are a relay in front of the door that strips the origin, or onion routing for the knock. *Cost to close:* a relay is one more Worker and a small VM for the exit, perhaps $20–50 a month; an onion layer is a design project before it is a cost.

**Offline.** *Kind: a gap, by our choice.* The expensive half of the proof is prepared offline; the showing half needs the runtime, because that is where the release judges the wire. OpenAC's `Show` is cheap enough to run on a phone. A local judge evaluating a cached mandate on the device would let the agent present without the runtime; it moves the judge off the custodied side, which is a real trade we have not made. *Cost to close:* none in infrastructure; the cost is the security argument.

**A door's receipts.** *Kind: closed.* A receipt keeps its parties only for the window in which a dispute could need them (thirty days at the live door), then collapses to the kind of decision, its effect, its codes and the decision it belongs to. The row's hash in the chain never changes, so the chain still vouches for a record the door no longer holds. The payment ticket binds the admission receipt, never the person.

**Issuer.** *Kind: closed.* A door names the issuers it accepts and the verifier is handed only their facets; a presentation under any other facet is refused before a byte of proof is read. The organization's own agent issues to its members, keeps the record, vouches on its own row, and the scheduled projector keeps that row fresh — the row used to go stale a day after an operator last anchored it by hand. The issuing playbook is its own archetype, assigned only to an organization that has published a facet, so turning issuing on for one organization changes no other.

**Transport.** *Kind: closed.* A zero-knowledge proof bundle is tens of kilobytes and the edge of the public internet caps an HTTP header at sixteen, so a knock has two steps by specification: the presentation is posted first and held under a reference the door derives, unverified, for ten minutes, and the knock cites that reference. Nothing is verified until the knock, and the receipt keeps the digest, never the bundle.

**The signer patch.** *Kind: a gap in OpenAC's API, carried otherwise.* The published `openac-sdk` takes the device private key as a value in the prover's process; we run a forty-line patch that lets `present()` take a signer callback instead, so the key can stay in the key service. The patch is applied on every install, refuses a high-S or malformed signature, and is written up for upstream. *Cost to close:* a review upstream.

**The four-pattern limit.** *Kind: infrastructure.* The 1k circuit matches at most four claim-or-predicate patterns, so a membership credential that proves two predicates carries two claims. The 2k keys lift it at the cost of larger proving keys and a slower Prepare; we take them when the prover's time budget allows. *Cost to close:* storage for the keys and seconds per proof.

**Settings relaxed for testing.** *Kind: configuration, before real users.* The door's nullifier window is five minutes rather than the hour the design calls for, so the gates can re-run; the holder's key-service token lasts ninety days; the evidence that releases a device signature is minted by the ceremony's own key rather than the Home runtime's; the payment ticket and predicate windows are fifteen minutes; receipts keep their parties thirty days. Every one is on a list with the value it should take and where it is set. *Cost to close:* an hour, and a decision per row.

Fourteen items. Four are closed. Three are OpenAC's and we carry the property another way until its circuits land. Four are infrastructure or configuration — things to deploy or set, with the design already in place. Three are gaps of ours: two we are building, and one we have chosen not to close because the trade is real.
